Getting My Wii Running Again

Jul 24, 2026 3 min read wii · wiilink · retro

I recently unearthed my old Wii from the depths of a storage closet. Shockingly, it still works flawlessly. I booted it up, played around with the menus, and listened to that ambient background hum before a specific, slightly painful childhood memory came rushing back. See back in the day, when I was in grade school, I was gifted this Wii from... I can't really remember. But, this Wii was EVERYTHING to me and my brother.

However, buying games was somewhat pricey, and it could quickly compound - and my dad realised that pretty quickly. So he went and jailbroke it. Well, he didn't, he sent it off to a nearby shop and the guy there jailbroke it and sold games to my dad for fractions of the price. I didn't know any of that, I was a kid, nor did I care - I was able to play any game I wanted. Anyway, I say this because I distinctly remember updating the Wii, breaking the jailbroken firmware, and my brother getting really mad at me. We couldn't play the cracked games anymore. There ends the flashback.

Cut to today. I'm nineteen, freshly finished with my first year of uni, armed with years of programming knowledge, and staring down a wide-open summer. I realized it was finally time to fix up this old console and atone for my grave childhood mistake.

The actual revival process was surprisingly straightforward. What seemed like dark magic performed by a sketchy shop owner a decade ago now just took an SD card, a laptop, and a few minutes of reading documentation. The homebrew setup was seamless, but while poking around the modding scene, I stumbled across a project that completely derailed my afternoon: WiiLink.

For the uninitiated, WiiLink is a fan-made open-source project dedicated to resurrecting the Wii's original online services long after Nintendo officially pulled the plug on WiiConnect24. The results are nothing short of incredible. Suddenly, the disc drive slot was pulsing with that iconic neon blue light again, signaling incoming mail. Channels I never even knew existed, largely because they were previously locked to Japan or Europe, were suddenly sitting right there on my menu, fully translated and functional.

Did you know there was a Demae Channel that let you order actual, real-world food delivery through your console? Back in the late 2000s, families in Japan were ordering pizza and sushi using their Wiimotes, complete with a little delivery driver Mii rolling across the screen. WiiLink brought the interface back to life just so users today can experience how wildly ahead of its time it was. Then there was the Wii Room, a bizarre but charming social app where you could watch TV and video content in a virtual living room alongside your friends' Miis. And it works in the UK, like actually works — through JustEat.

Even the staple channels feel magical to use again. Seeing the Forecast Channel's 3D globe populate with real-time, accurate weather data today, accompanied by that incredibly relaxing ambient soundtrack, is a surreal experience. It transforms the console from an isolated retro gaming box back into the living, breathing multimedia hub it was always designed to be.

It was weirdly jarring seeing the news about Iran on my Wii though.

It's frankly an amazing piece of software, and I'm genuinely in awe of the reverse-engineering community. They didn't just preserve the games; they preserved the atmosphere, the quirks, and the culture of the Wii era. It's one thing to emulate Mario Kart on a PC, but it's an entirely different feeling to sit on your living room floor, point a remote at the sensor bar, and watch a long-dead digital world come back to life. My childhood mistake might have cost my brother and me our game library back then, but fixing it today gave me a whole new appreciation for this little black box.
